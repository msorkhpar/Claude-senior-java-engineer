A shop chain's daily sales report is due at 07:00, Amsterdam time. From 29 March 2026 it arrives at 08:00, every day. On 25 October it is back at 07:00, and nobody touched it. The scheduler works out the next run like this:

java.time has two types for an amount of time. Duration is an exact number of seconds and nanoseconds. Period is a number of years, months and days on a calendar. Outputs below are from Java 21.0.12 with tzdata 2026b, run with -Duser.timezone=UTC; a run with -Duser.timezone=Asia/Tokyo printed the same lines, since nothing here reads the default zone.

Duration.ofDays(1) Is 24 Hours; Period.ofDays(1) Is the Next Calendar Day

Add each kind of day to a 07:00 run on the day before each clock change. What does the wall clock show?

Duration.ofDays(1) is 86,400 seconds; "days" is only a way to write them. Added to a ZonedDateTime, it moves the instant on by that much. 29 March has 23 hours in Amsterdam, so 24 hours after 07:00 on the 28th the clock reads 08:00, and every later run adds 24 hours to 08:00 until October's 25-hour day takes the hour back. Period.ofDays(1) changes the date and keeps the wall-clock time.

A report "every day at 07:00" and a renewal "in one month" are calendar amounts: use Period or plusDays. A session that expires "after 24 hours" and a timeout are elapsed time: use Duration. Mixed up the other way, a 24-hour reservation made with plusDays(1) lasts 25 hours one night a year and 23 another. A LocalDate has no seconds, so adding a Duration to it throws (see the Reference). Times inside the gap and the overlap themselves are unit Zoned and Offset Date and Time.

  1. Coding practice

    Keep the pharmacy's reminders and holds right through a clock change

    • Duration.ofDays(1) is 24 elapsed hours while Period.ofDays(1) is the same wall-clock time on the next date, so across a DST change on a ZonedDateTime they differ by an hour.

Period.between Returns Fields; Count Days With ChronoUnit.DAYS.between


An invoice was due on 31 January 2026, and the late fee is charged per day. The billing code reads the number of days late from Period.between(due, paid).getDays():




Period.between answers "how many whole years, months and days". getDays() is the last field of that answer, the days left over after the whole months. The customer who paid 31 days late was charged for 3, and the one who paid 364 days late for 30. Every test invoice had been paid within the month.


For a total in one unit, use ChronoUnit.DAYS.between(a, b) (or WEEKS, MONTHS, ...), or a.until(b, unit). Keep Period.between for answers in calendar fields: an age is Period.between(birth, today).getYears(), which a count of days divided by 365 gets wrong in the days before a birthday.

A Period Keeps the Fields It Was Given, So P14M Is Not P1Y2M


A plan catalogue finds plans by their term. One supplier sends P14M, and the catalogue holds P1Y2M:




A Period stores its three fields as given, and equals compares the fields. normalized() folds months into years, twelve to one, and balances their signs. It never touches the days: how many days make a month depends on the month, and a Period doesn't know which one it will be added to. So P1M and P30D stay different: from 1 February they give different dates. Normalize both sides before you compare two terms or use one as a map key.


Duration stores an exact length, so it has no such question: Duration.ofMinutes(90) and Duration.ofSeconds(5400) are equal and both print PT1H30M.

    • Schedules and calendar deadlines are Period amounts; expiries, timeouts and other elapsed time are Duration amounts.

Duration Prints Hours, Not Days, and Its Hours and Minutes Carry the Sign


toString() and parse() use ISO-8601: P, then date fields, then T and time fields. Expression, then result:



A Duration never prints days: its largest field is the hour. It accepts D on the way in and counts each day as 24 hours, but it refuses years, months and weeks. A config value P1M is a month to Period.parse and an error to Duration.parse; PT1M is one minute.


A night shift runs from 22:00 to 06:30, and a timesheet shows an overtime balance of minus 90 minutes:




between(start, end) subtracts, and a LocalTime has no next day, so the shift came out as minus 15½ hours. Where an end before the start means the next day, add Duration.ofDays(1) to a negative result. In a negative Duration, toHours() and toMinutesPart() (and toSecondsPart()) are all negative, so the first balance printed -1:-30. Print the sign once and the parts of abs(). Period.between with the later date first gives P-1M-3D, every field negative.


toHours() is the total; toHoursPart() is the hours left after whole days. A timesheet total of 30 hours printed with toHoursPart() shows 6.

    Coding practice
    

    Show the night team's overtime balance the way payroll reads it

      • 

    • toHours() is the total hours while toHoursPart() is the hours left after whole days.
      • 

    • The hours, minutes and seconds parts of a negative Duration are all negative, so a label is built from the sign once and the parts of abs().

Summary


  • Duration.ofDays(1) is 24 hours, Period.ofDays(1) is the next calendar day. Across a DST change they differ by an hour: schedules use Period, expiries and timeouts Duration.
  • Period.between(...).getDays() is a field, not a total. Count with ChronoUnit.DAYS.between; use Period.between for ages and other calendar answers.
  • A Period compares field by field. P14M is not P1Y2M until normalized, and normalized() never turns days into months.
  • A Duration prints hours, not days, and its hours and minutes carry its sign. between doesn't wrap midnight; format a negative amount from its abs().

Design Note: Why Two Amount Types


An amount that held "1 month and 3 hours" would need a date before it had a length, so it could not be compared, summed or stored as a number. java.time splits it in two. Duration is a number: it sorts, adds and divides, and it is the same length everywhere. Period is an instruction to the calendar: it means what people mean by "next month", and it has no length until it meets a date. Duration.ofDays looks like a calendar amount and isn't; that is the opening scheduler's bug.


Every caller has to decide which question each amount answers, because plus(TemporalAmount) accepts both, and a LocalDate refuses a Duration only at run time.
