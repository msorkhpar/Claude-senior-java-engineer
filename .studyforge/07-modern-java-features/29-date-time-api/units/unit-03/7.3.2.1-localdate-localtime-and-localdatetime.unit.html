A customer's first loan instalment was due on 31 January 2026, and her contract says "the last day of each month". Her schedule asks for 28 February, 28 March, 28 April and 28 May. The code that built it adds one month to the previous due date. Nothing threw, and every date is valid.

LocalDate holds a date, LocalTime a time of day, LocalDateTime both. None of them holds a zone or an offset, and all three are immutable values (unit Overview of the java.time package). Every output below comes from Java 21 (tzdata 2026b), run with -Duser.timezone=UTC; a second run with -Duser.timezone=Asia/Tokyo printed the same lines, because no example reads the default zone.

A LocalDateTime Is a Wall-Clock Reading, and the Same One in Two Zones Is Two Moments

A reminders table stores 2026-03-27T09:00 in a LocalDateTime column. A job turns it into a moment with the zone of the server it runs on:

The two cities' clocks show 09:00 at different moments, here five hours apart, not the usual six, because New York had moved its clocks on 8 March and Amsterdam would only on 29 March (unit Zoned and Offset Date and Time). Instant.from refuses because the value doesn't say which clock it was read from.

Moving the job from the Amsterdam server to a New York one makes every reminder fire five or six hours late, and no row changes. Store a moment as an Instant (unit Instant and Timestamps). Keep a LocalDateTime for what is meant as a local reading, such as opening hours or an appointment, and keep the zone beside it.

plusMonths Clamps to a Shorter Month's Last Day, So Step From the First Date

The loan schedule, built both ways:

plusMonths changes the month and keeps the day if the new month has it. February 2026 has no 31st, so the result is clamped to the 28th. Nothing in a LocalDate records the 31st, so every later step starts from the 28th, and adding a month then subtracting it gives 28 January.

first.plusMonths(n) clamps only the month that needs it. Compute each date of a series from its first date and its index, never from the previous result. Chained, this customer pays up to three days early every month.

The Last Day of a Month Comes From an Adjuster, Not a Day Number

A second customer's first instalment is due on 28 February 2026, also "the last day of each month". Stepping from the first date gives the 28th every month. Compare an adjuster and a fixed day number:

withDayOfMonth sets a field and checks the result: April has no 31st, so it throws DateTimeException, and a schedule job that reaches April stops. TemporalAdjusters.lastDayOfMonth(), applied with with(...), uses the month's length instead of a day number.

A contract is end-of-month when its first date is its month's last day: date.getDayOfMonth() == date.lengthOfMonth(). A test for day 31 misses the end of every 30-day month and of February.

  1. Coding practice

    Keep a loan's due dates on the day its contract names

    • The last day of a month comes from TemporalAdjusters.lastDayOfMonth() or the month's length, not from a fixed day number; whether a date is a month end depends on its month and year.
    • plusMonths clamps the day to a shorter month's last day and forgets the original day, so chained month steps drift and don't reverse; each date of a series is computed from the first one.

LocalDate.parse and LocalDate.of Refuse Dates That Don't Exist


A partner sends due dates as text. Five lines of its file:




LocalDate.parse(text) uses DateTimeFormatter.ISO_LOCAL_DATE with its strict resolver: two digits for month and day, nothing after, and no date the calendar doesn't have. LocalDate.of(2026, 2, 29) throws DateTimeException for the same reason. Where SimpleDateFormat turned 30 February into 2 March (unit Limitations of the Legacy Date and Calendar Classes), the import now stops at the line with DateTimeParseException. The other two refusals are about format: trim the text, or read 2026-2-5 with a pattern of your own (unit DateTimeFormatter). A pattern from ofPattern resolves smartly, not strictly: uuuu-M-d reads 2026-4-31 as 30 April. Add withResolverStyle(ResolverStyle.STRICT), and keep uuuu, since strict yyyy refuses even 2026-2-5.


datesUntil Leaves Out Its End Date


The March report collects the payments of every day from 1 to 31 March:




datesUntil(end) excludes end, like subList or IntStream.range, so the report missed the 31st's payments. For a period given by its first and last day, pass last.plusDays(1). A hotel stay already ends on an exclusive date, the check-out day, whose night isn't charged; adding a day there bills one night too many.

Summary


  • A LocalDateTime is a wall-clock reading, not a moment. Turning it into one needs a zone, and two zones give two instants.
  • plusMonths clamps to the last day of a shorter month. Chained steps drift and don't reverse; compute each date from the first one.
  • "The last day of the month" is TemporalAdjusters.lastDayOfMonth(). withDayOfMonth(31) throws in a 30-day month.
  • LocalDate.parse(text) and LocalDate.of refuse impossible dates. A pattern of your own needs the strict resolver to do the same.
  • datesUntil excludes its end. An inclusive period needs last.plusDays(1).

Design Note: Why plusMonths Clamps Instead of Failing


plusMonths could have thrown on 31 January plus one month, as withDayOfMonth(31) does in April. The JDK chose the month's last valid day, as its javadoc says. That suits the common case, where "a month later" means "the same day if it exists, otherwise the month's end", and no caller has to handle an exception for February.


A clamped date doesn't record that it was clamped, so month steps don't add up or reverse, and the bug is a valid, wrong date rather than an exception. A series must keep its first date and its own end-of-month rule, since a LocalDate holds neither.
