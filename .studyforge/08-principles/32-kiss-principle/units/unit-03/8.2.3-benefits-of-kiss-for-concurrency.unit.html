A box office sells 500 seats per show. Its ticket counter keeps two numbers, seats free and seats held in a basket, and a health check pages the on-call engineer whenever free + held isn't 500. A few times a week it fires, with 499 or 501. Nobody can reproduce it, and the counter's concurrency test passes. The counter was written lock-free, for speed:

The simple version has two int fields and the same three methods, each synchronized:

A Tool That Runs Every Schedule of a Small Model

A test runs the threads in whatever order the machine picks. To see every order, we model each method as a list of atomic steps, actions no other thread can interleave with, and let a small explorer try every schedule: at each point, any thread that isn't finished may take its next step.

A schedule is one complete order of every thread's steps; a state is the shared values plus each thread's position and locals.

Each Step Other Threads Can Run Between Multiplies the Schedules: 6 for synchronized, 1,124 for Lock-Free

The model has 4 seats (CAP), and shared holds {free, held, wrong sums seen}. The lock-free hold() is three steps, because another thread can run between any two of them, and the check is two:

In SyncTickets no other thread runs inside a method, so each method is one step. A third version, SnapshotTickets (one of unit Applying KISS in concurrent programming's constructs), keeps both counts in one immutable record Count(int free, int held) and swaps it with one compareAndSet on an AtomicReference<Count>: its hold() is two steps (read the record, swap in a new one or retry; the model uses a version number for the reference) and its check is one read. One check runs against one, two and three holders (every output on this page is from Java 21 on a 20-thread x86-64 machine):

With one step per method, three threads have 3! = 6 orders. Each extra step multiplies that: steps of 3, 3 and 2 alone give 8!/(3!·3!·2!) = 560 orders, and each failed compareAndSet adds a retry: the lock-free version has 1,124 schedules for two holds and a check, and 2.75 million for three.

520 of the 1,124 lock-free schedules break the invariant, in both directions. A check that reads free after a hold took a seat, and held before the hold counted it, sees 3 of 4; one that reads free before and held after sees 5. Each AtomicInteger is correct on its own. The invariant spans two of them, and nothing makes the pair change at once.

A Stress Test Samples the Likely Schedules, So It Seldom Hits the Broken Ones

The counter's concurrency test runs two holds and a check, released together by a latch, and the check must see 500. It ran 10,000 times per version, five times over:

The model says 520 of 1,124 schedules are broken; the test fails about 3 times in 1,000. The schedules aren't equally likely. The gap between compareAndSet and incrementAndGet is a few instructions, and the threads leave the latch at slightly different moments, so most runs execute one call after another. A test run once per build fails about one build in 340.

The zeros for the other two versions show only that these runs met no broken schedule. That none exists comes from the model, which tried every schedule of four seats and up to four threads, taking each step as atomic and each write as visible at once: what the lock and the AtomicReference provide.

In the Simple Design the Race Has No Schedule, and Its Correctness Argument Is One Sentence

A race the model finds can be forced in a real test by pausing one thread at the dangerous point, as unit Data races and how to prevent them does; a pause after any step is a schedule the real system can produce. Here the midHold hook pauses hold() between its two writes, and the health check runs meanwhile:

The lock-free check returns 499 every time it meets a paused hold; only a check made after the hold sees 500. The synchronized check can't run until the hold is done: the paused thread holds the monitor, and total() needs it.

The correctness argument for SyncTickets is one sentence: after the constructor, every read and write of free and held happens while holding this object's monitor, so no thread sees one changed without the other. A reviewer checks it by finding every use of the two fields: all of them are in three synchronized methods. For SnapshotTickets it is also one sentence: the state is one immutable record, and every change replaces it with one compareAndSet, so a reader gets the old record or the new one, never half of each. The lock-free version has no such sentence. Its argument would have to cover every point between two atomic calls, and the first one already fails.

A Thread Dump Names a Monitor's Holder; a Hand-Rolled Lock Shows Only Busy Threads

Suppose the team fixes the torn read with a spin lock instead of synchronized, because "it makes no kernel call, so it's cheaper than synchronized". SpinTickets has the two int fields of SyncTickets and wraps every method in this lock, with unlock() in a finally:

By the same one-sentence argument it is correct, and the stress test agrees: 0 failures in five runs of 10,000. Then one holder stalls inside hold(), with the midHold hook standing for an audit write to a disk that stopped answering, while another counter thread and the health check call in. Printed through unit Thread lifecycle and states's Dump helper (state, the lock waited for and its holder, CPU used in a window of about 200 ms):

With the spin lock, the waiting threads look like threads at work: RUNNABLE, a core each, no lock and no owner. With synchronized they are BLOCKED, and the line names the thread to look at. jcmd <pid> Thread.print says the same. In this excerpt the first two threads are from the spin-lock run and the last two from the synchronized run; ... marks left-out frames:

The address after waiting to lock matches one - locked line, in the stack of the thread that holds the monitor, and that stack shows where it is stuck. The spin lock's holder has SpinTickets.hold in its stack and no - locked line: to find it, you read every stack in the dump for a thread inside SpinTickets but not in lock(). An AtomicBoolean records only true, not who set it, so the deadlock detector of unit Best practices for safe concurrent programming can't see a spin lock either.

A monitor also releases itself: a synchronized method or block that ends with an exception gives up the lock (JLS §8.4.3.6, §14.19). A hand-written lock is released only where every caller wrote the finally. Two methods throw while holding a lock, and another thread then tries to get in:

Simplicity Costs Throughput Under Contention: Measure It Against the Load You Have

The argument for the lock-free counter was speed. Here each thread holds and releases a seat in a loop for one second, after a one-second warm-up, and the numbers are pairs per second, all threads together:

That is one of three runs. At 16 threads the two atomics made 9.7 to 9.8 million pairs a second and synchronized 4.1 to 7.6 million: the broken version was 1.3 to 2.4 times as fast. In all three runs, at 4 and 16 threads, the spin lock written to be cheaper was the slowest, and the record was slower than synchronized, probably because every failed compareAndSet throws away a new record and tries again.

A counter that takes 2,000 holds a second at its busiest uses at most about a two-thousandth of what synchronized handled here. As in unit Definition and purpose of KISS principle, the stated load decides, and here the simple design meets it with that much room. A loop that does nothing but take a lock overstates contention; a real request spends most of its time elsewhere.

Summary

  • Each step other threads can run between multiplies the schedules. Two holds and a check have 6 schedules when each method is one step, and 1,124 in the lock-free counter, 520 of them broken.
  • A stress test samples the likely schedules. It failed about 3 times in 1,000 against a design the model shows is wrong in 520 of its 1,124 schedules.
  • In the simple design the race has no schedule. A paused hold() makes the lock-free check return 499 every time; the synchronized check waits and returns 500. Its correctness argument is one sentence a reviewer can check.
  • A dump names a monitor's holder. Threads waiting on a spin lock show as RUNNABLE and burn CPU, with no owner and no - locked line.
  • The simple design costs throughput under contention. Measure it against the load you have before trading its properties for speed.

Challenges

  1. Add release() to the model of the lock-free counter. How many schedules do one hold, one release and one check have, and which wrong sums can the check see?
  2. The box office adds a third count, sold. Write the correctness sentence for a synchronized version and for a record version. Which one still fits in a sentence if a sale must also update a revenue total kept in another object?
  3. A service is stuck, and the dump shows 40 threads RUNNABLE in one class's acquire(), none owning anything. How do you find the holder, and what would you change so the next dump tells you?
  4. Rerun the throughput loop with a few microseconds of work outside the lock in each iteration. At what amount of work does the difference between the versions stop mattering?

Design Note: Why the JVM Knows Who Holds a Monitor

Every Java object can be locked, and the JVM implements that lock itself, so it can always tell which thread owns a monitor. That is what lets a dump print - locked and waiting to lock with matching addresses, lets ThreadMXBean name an owner, and lets the deadlock detector find a cycle. The java.util.concurrent locks keep their owner in AbstractOwnableSynchronizer, whose javadoc says the value is there to help "monitor access and provide diagnostics". No dump, ThreadMXBean call or deadlock check can name the holder of a lock built from an AtomicBoolean.

The price is taking the JDK's semantics as they are: a monitor has no timeout and no tryLock, and a ReentrantLock needs a finally in every caller.

A bank's fraud monitor watches each customer's money from its own threads while transfers run. Twice last month it froze an account because the customer's total had dropped by exactly the amount of a transfer between their own checking and savings, and a minute later the total was back. The monitor reads total() and statement(); the app calls transfer(...).
