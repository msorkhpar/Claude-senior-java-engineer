A warehouse service moves stock between 100 bins on four threads. A dashboard audit adds up the bins and raises an alert when the total isn't 100,000. The alert fires on almost every audit, yet the closing count is always exactly 100,000. Every bin has its own lock, and every move takes both bins' locks in a fixed order.

Every output is from Java 21 on one 20-core machine; timings come from plain loops, not a benchmark harness.

A Lock per Bin Keeps Every Move Right and Every Total Wrong

No field is read or written without its lock, and the lock order rules out deadlock. Four threads make random moves for one second while a fifth audits in a loop:

Three runs gave 233,571 to 247,366 wrong totals out of 243,962 to 262,097 audits.

Say the audit has added up bins 0 to 40 when a move takes 3 units from bin 70 to bin 12. Bin 12 was counted before the move and bin 70 will be counted after it, so the audit comes out 3 short. A move the other way makes it 3 too high. Each lock protects one bin, and the total is a fact about all 100 at once, which no single lock protects. Inside this design the direct cure is for total() to hold all 100 locks, taken in order, for the whole sum.

One Lock Makes the Total Right; One Owner Thread Also Keeps Readers From Waiting

The first fix deletes code: drop the lock array and make move and total synchronized. The second gives the bins an owner: a single-thread executor is the only thread that touches qty after construction, so the array needs no lock (unit Best practices for safe concurrent programming), and after each move it publishes a copy that nothing changes:

The same race, with the movers waiting on each Future and the audit reading stock().total():

Both are right in all three runs. The volatile write publishes the finished Stock (unit Proper synchronization to ensure memory visibility), and a reader holds one whole state, from before or after any move, for as long as it likes. Stock copies the array. A read-only view of live state would not do: the javadoc of Collections.unmodifiableMap(m) says its reads go through to m, so it changes under the reader with every move.

The two fixes differ in who waits. With one lock, an audit waits for a move and a move waits for an audit. With an owner, readers never wait: 12 million audits against 2.3 million. Moves cost more: each is a hand-off to another thread and back, and the owner did 340,000 to 490,000 moves a second against the one lock's 6.4 to 9 million. When nobody needs reads that never block, one lock is the simpler choice.

A decision belongs on the owner too. A caller that checks stock().of(from) >= n and then submits a move without the check is the check-then-act of unit Data races and how to prevent them. Two callers asked to take 3 units from a bin holding 3, while the owner was still busy with an earlier task:

Checked on the caller, both moves read the same snapshot (bin 0 holds 3) and passed. Inside the task, the check runs on the only thread that changes the bins, so nothing comes between it and the move.

A Snapshot of 100,000 Bins Copies 800 KB on Every Move

The same race with 100,000 bins, plus an owner with no snapshot that runs each audit as a task submitted to the owner:

Every move now copies 800 KB, and the owner managed 10,500 to 12,900 moves a second over three runs. A snapshot suits small state. For large state that changes often, send the read to the owner as a task, which then waits its turn behind the moves, or use one lock (790,000 to 2.5 million moves a second here).

A compareAndSet Loop That Reads the Value Once Spins Forever; incrementAndGet Leaves No Loop to Get Wrong

A counter whose author read that atomics beat locks, with its retry loop written by hand:

Spot the problem before the run. Four threads, a million increments each, two seconds to finish:

Ten runs out of ten ended this way, with the count between 1,000,000 and 1,000,255. seen is read once, before the loop. The first time another thread wins, count moves past seen and never comes back, so every later compareAndSet(seen, …) fails: the thread spins at 100 % until the JVM exits. A retry loop has to read the value again on every failure, as updateAndGet() does inside the JDK (unit Atomicity violations and ensuring atomic operations shows it). incrementAndGet() and a synchronized method around count++ leave no loop to write, three runs each:

incrementAndGet() took 41 to 49 ms and synchronized 246 to 294 ms, for four threads doing nothing but counting. For a counter that is the hot spot, unit Atomicity violations and ensuring atomic operations measures LongAdder.

A Double-Checked Flag Can Say Loaded Before the Value Is; a synchronized Getter Can't

Exchange rates are loaded on first use. The author wanted no lock once they are loaded, and set the flag first so that no second caller starts another load:

The plain version locks every call and uses the value itself as the flag:

Thread A calls rates(), and its load is held open while thread B calls rates() and for 500 ms after. A second run makes the first load throw. volatile ref is the double-checked locking of unit Synchronization and memory consistency: one volatile field holding the map, read once into a local.

In all three runs the flag said "loaded" while the map was still null: the flag and the map are two variables. B read the flag without the lock, so it never waited for the load. When the load threw, the flag stayed true and the map null, and every later call returns null until the process restarts. In SyncRates there is only the map: null means "not loaded", a failed load leaves it null, and the next call tries again. The volatile version also has a single variable.

The lock costs time. Eight threads that do nothing but call rates() 10 million times each:

Keep the synchronized getter until a profile shows it, then switch to the volatile reference.

Summary

  • A lock per object keeps each object right and leaves every fact about several objects unprotected. Making the total right inside that design means holding every lock at once.
  • One lock fixes it by deleting code; one owner thread with an immutable snapshot fixes it and keeps readers from waiting. The owner costs a hand-off per write and a copy per snapshot, and decisions about the state are made on the owner, not on a snapshot.
  • A hand-written compareAndSet loop that doesn't re-read the value spins forever after its first lost race. incrementAndGet() or synchronized is correct with no loop of your own.
  • A double-checked flag beside the value can say "loaded" while the value is `null`, and a failed load leaves it so for good. A synchronized getter whose value is its own flag is right; the volatile-reference version of unit Synchronization and memory consistency is the fix when the lock shows in a profile.

Challenges

  1. The warehouse also needs "move 10 units from bin 4 to whichever of bins 7, 8 and 9 has the least stock". Write it for one lock and for the owner thread. Which version needs a new idea?
  2. At what number of bins does the snapshot's copy cost more than the hand-off on your machine? Race four movers against an auditor as this page does, then try a snapshot built from a persistent map instead of a copy.
  3. Your counter must also record the time of the last increment, and readers want both values together. Which of AtomicLong, synchronized and an immutable pair behind an AtomicReference (unit Atomicity violations and ensuring atomic operations) do you choose, and why?

Design Note: Simple Means Fewer Shared Variables, Not Fewer Lines

Unit Definition and purpose of KISS principle judged simplicity by the code you read. With threads, the measure is how many shared mutable variables a reader has to keep in mind at once, and in how many places they change. OwnedBins is longer than LockedBins, yet once it is built the only variable two threads share is stock, and only the owner writes it. FlagRates is longer than SyncRates because it keeps a second variable in step with the first, and that second variable is the bug.

The price of that design is throughput: one lock admits one thread at a time and one owner thread does one thing at a time, so neither scales with cores the way the broken per-bin locks did. Unit Benefits of KISS for concurrency counts what the simpler design buys in return: fewer interleavings to reason about and test.

Finish the two TODOs in src/main/java/practice/Wallets.java:
