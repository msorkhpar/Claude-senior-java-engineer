Finance closes the month and finds that every invoice since Tuesday's deploy is a whole number of euros: 119 charged where the price list gives 118.99. Tuesday's deploy held one change, asked for by the sales team: show whole euros in the daily sales summary. The summary's test was updated with it and passed, and so did finance's, whose prices were all whole euros.

SRP is usually quoted as "a class should have one reason to change".

A Reason to Change Is an Actor Who Asks for It

A reason to change is an actor, a person or team whose requests change the class. Robert C. Martin later restated SRP that way: a module should be responsible to one, and only one, actor. This class has two:

Each team has a test. Every example on this page runs the same two:

Both pass. Now the sales request arrives: whole euros in the summary. The summary prints through money(), so that is where the developer makes the change, and the sales test now expects EUR 119:

Which test fails?

Sales got its whole euros, and finance's total moved. To the developer, money() was the summary's formatting; to total(), it was the rounding rule for every line and for the amount charged. One private method answered to two actors, so a request from one changed the other's result.

Here finance's test uses an amount that isn't a whole number of euros, and the build fails. With the whole-euro prices of the opening story, it passes and the change ships: each line is rounded to whole euros before the discount and VAT, the total is rounded again, and any invoice whose lines or total weren't whole euros is charged a different amount.

To find a class's actors, ask whose request would change each public method, then look at what methods of different actors share. Here total is finance's, summaryLine is the sales team's, and they share money().

Moving Methods into Separate Classes Fixes Nothing While the Actors Share a Helper

The usual refactoring splits InvoiceDesk into InvoicePricing.total(lines) and SalesSummary.line(id, lines), makes Line a top-level record, and moves the rounding to a utility both can reuse. The two classes are the ones shown in the next section, with Money.round in place of their private rounding methods:

The summary prints through Money.round, so the sales request changes it. The checks, rewired to the two classes, print:

Money answers to both actors, as money() did. A split counts only when the code a request changes belongs to the actor who asked.

Before the request, cents() and shown() were the same line of code. They were two rules that happened to agree: the precision finance charges in, and the precision the sales team reads in. Splitting by Verb Instead of by Actor Moves the Rule into Every Caller

Here finance's pricing is split into four classes, each with one method and its own test (LineSum.of and Cents.round are built like these two):

The checkout wires them, holding one of each in the fields sum, discount, vat and cents. A year later the sales team's quote PDF needs the same price, and its developer wires the same four classes:

Each step's test passes. Finance's rule that the discount threshold applies to the net amount, before VAT, is in none of the classes: it is in the order of the calls, and each caller wrote its own. The quote added VAT first, so a net 90.00 order crossed the threshold, and the customer was quoted 102.60 and charged 108.00. A scan of every net amount up to 200.00 finds the two disagree on each one from 83.34 to 99.99.

The four steps have one actor, finance, so they belong in one class. InvoicePricing from the previous section is that class, and both callers ask it:

The number of methods doesn't measure a responsibility: a pricing class with twenty methods, all changed at finance's request, has one reason to change, and a step earns a class of its own when it has an actor of its own.

Summary

  • A reason to change is an actor, a person or team whose requests change the class. Two actors in one class meet in the code they share, and a change for one moves the other's results.
  • Splitting a class doesn't help while both actors still call one helper: a Money class with two actors fails the same way.
  • Each actor owns its rules, even ones that look identical today. Two equal lines that answer to two actors are two rules.
  • Splitting by verb puts the actor's rule into the callers: the order of the steps lives in each caller, and two callers diverge. One actor's steps stay in one class, however many methods that takes.

Challenges

  1. A Customer entity is changed by the marketing team (newsletter flags), the billing team (payment terms) and the support team (notes). It is one database row. Which parts would you split, and what would still be shared?
  2. An interviewer hands you a 2,000-line OrderService and asks you to apply SRP. Before moving any code, what would you ask, and which history in version control would show you the actors?
  3. Find a utility class in a codebase you know and list the callers of each method by team. Which methods have more than one actor, and what would a change to each of them break?

Design Note: Why the Principle Names People, Not Code

"One reason to change" is easy to read as "does one thing", and that reading produces the verb classes of the last section. Naming the actor moves the question from the code to the organisation: who asks for changes to this line? The code can't answer it, so a design built this way rests on knowing the teams and their rules, and has to move when they do. Today the quote calls finance's total() because the price is finance's rule. If sales later gets discount rules of its own for quotes, total() has two actors, it becomes the shared helper of the first section, and the quote needs a pricing class of its own.
