A customer's prepaid wallet holds 100 credits. She buys an 80-credit item, her monthly plan renews for 30, and afterwards her balance reads 70. Checkout, gifts and renewals each charge the wallet, and each passed review. Checkout and gifts lock the wallet around the charge; the renewal job, written last, doesn't.

"Hold the wallet's lock from the read to the write" is knowledge in the sense of unit Definition and purpose of DRY principle, and here every call site has to repeat it. Every output below is from Java 21 on a 20-core machine.

A Lock Written at Each Call Site Is Missing at the Site That Forgets It

The wallet offers a read and a write, each synchronized, and leaves the charge to its callers:

Checkout follows the class comment, and Gifts.send has the same body. The renewal job asks the tax service for the tax between its read and its write:

To get the bad order on every run, the test's tax service waits on a latch, and a checkout runs while the renewal waits:

The renewal kept 100 in a local variable, and its setBalance(70) overwrote the checkout's charge. Each method of Wallet is atomic; the charge is a check followed by an act across two of them (unit Monitors and synchronized blocks). The renewal takes the monitor for each call and lets go in between, so checkout's lock, held from read to write, never keeps it out.

With an instant tax service and no latch, seven checkout threads and one renewal thread drain a million-credit wallet, ten times:

Every run lost credits, as did 30 more, by different amounts each run. The losses are debits made by the locked checkouts and overwritten by the renewal. The class's public setter is what lets any caller compose the charge.

The charge moves into Wallet, and the setter goes:

Checkout and gifts become return wallet.tryDebit(price);. The renewal asks for the tax first, then calls wallet.tryDebit(due). The same latch and the same order:

The renewal still waits for the tax service at the same moment, but it holds no balance while it waits. The ten stress runs end with runs with lost credits: 0 of 10, and so do three more sets of ten. Clean runs are a sample; the reason it holds is in one file: after construction, every method that reads or changes credits is synchronized on the wallet, with the lock named next to the field as in unit Best practices for safe concurrent programming.

Deleting setBalance also tells you where the old rule was written. Compiling the old call sites against the new Wallet:

javac lists all three call sites, the two locked ones and the renewal. A shared withLock(wallet, ...) helper would remove the repeated synchronized lines but none of these errors: each caller would still read, decide and write, and a caller that skips the helper still compiles. What one fix in the owning class buys later is unit Benefits of DRY for concurrency.

An Action Across Two Wallets Needs One Owner for Both Balances

A gift is a debit from one wallet and a credit to another, two calls, and the reconciliation job sums every wallet while gifts are in flight. Ann and Bo, with 100 credits each, send each other one credit a million times while an auditor sums their balances, first with Wallet as it is:

then with one class that owns every balance:

Two compound actions span both wallets: the gift, between its debit and its credit, and the audit, between its two reads. Both 199 and 201 are audits whose two reads straddled part of a gift: one that landed between a debit and its credit sees a credit missing, and one that read Ann before a gift from her and Bo after it counts the credit twice. Each wallet's lock covers one balance, and the rule "the total doesn't change" covers two, so its lock belongs to the object that owns both.

The cost is that every charge, gift and audit in the process now waits for one monitor. Fixed-order locking of both wallets (unit Monitors and synchronized blocks) keeps unrelated gifts parallel, but an audit must then take every wallet's lock.

Summary

  • A lock written at each call site is missing at the site that forgets it. One unlocked renewal wrote back a stale balance, overwriting debits made by the locked checkouts in every stress run.
  • The compound action belongs in the class that owns the lock. With tryDebit the same forced order declines the renewal, and deleting the setter makes javac list every caller that composed the charge.
  • An action across two objects needs one owner for both. Two thread-safe wallets still showed wrong totals to nine audits in ten; one class with one lock showed none, at the price of one monitor for all wallets.

Challenges

  1. Product asks for "pay what the wallet covers, the rest by card". Write it as a caller of balance() and tryDebit(), then as a method of Wallet. What can the caller version charge when a checkout runs in between?
  2. Wallets serializes every wallet in the process. Measure checkouts per second with 1, 8 and 32 threads, then try one lock per wallet with ordered locking for transfers. What does the audit cost in each design?
  3. Find a class in your code whose Javadoc tells callers which lock to hold. Which of its callers would javac list if you removed the methods that make the rule necessary?

Design Note: Why ConcurrentMap Has putIfAbsent

The synchronized collections made each call atomic and left compound actions to their callers, with the collection itself as the lock; their javadoc still tells callers to lock it for iteration. Java 5's ConcurrentMap moved the common compound actions into the interface (putIfAbsent, replace, remove(key, value)), and Java 8 added compute and merge, where the caller passes the decision as a function, which ConcurrentHashMap runs atomically.

A class can offer only the compound actions its authors foresaw, so a new one means a new method. Accepting a function instead runs the caller's code while the map holds a lock of its own, and ConcurrentHashMap's javadoc asks that the function be "short and simple" and not modify the map.
