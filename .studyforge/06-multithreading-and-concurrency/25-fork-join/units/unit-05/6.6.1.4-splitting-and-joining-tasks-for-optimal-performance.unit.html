The nightly revaluation prices 4 million positions on a ForkJoinPool(8). Its task is the SumTask of unit ForkJoinPool and RecursiveTask/RecursiveAction with a pricing call in the loop, and its threshold gives each worker one leaf. Then the desk added options: 5% of the positions, sorted to the end of the file, each 200 times the work of a bond. The job now takes as long as it does on one thread, the totals are right, and the CPU graph shows one busy core for most of the run.

Unit ForkJoinPool and RecursiveTask/RecursiveAction measured the threshold on work where every row costs the same. This unit is about work where they don't.

Uneven Work Needs Many More Leaves Than Workers

The cost model, and the threshold for a given number of leaves per worker (ex/Uneven.java):

Before reading on: with one leaf per worker, which worker gets the options, and what do the other seven do once their leaf is done?

The last leaf holds all 200,000 options: 800 million of the job's 876 million steps. Since each task forks its left half and computes its right half itself, that leaf runs on the worker that took the root. The other seven finish their bonds and go idle, and the job lasts as long as that leaf.

Stealing can't fix this. A thief takes only a queued task, and a leaf that has started is a plain loop on one thread that nothing can take half of. An uneven job needs leaves small enough to spread the expensive rows over many of them. Four leaves per worker, the target the JDK's parallel streams use, put the options in two leaves: 567 ms. Sixteen spread them over seven: 224 ms. The time stops falling at 64 per worker, 163 ms; 256 per worker gave 160.

Size from the pool you run in, pool.getParallelism(). A threshold computed from Runtime.availableProcessors() on a 4-core build server, then run in production's pool of 16, gives a quarter of the leaves it was meant to. How many leaves are enough depends on how narrow the expensive region is, so measure with production-shaped data; a uniform test file shows no difference at all.

  1. Coding practice

    Give every worker of the settlement pool several pieces

    • A started leaf is never shared, so uneven work needs leaves small enough that the costly items spread over many of them; one or four leaves per worker leave workers idle.
    • The number of leaves is sized from the parallelism of the pool the task runs in, not from the machine's processor count.

Problem statement

The settlement job nets the day's trades on whatever ForkJoinPool the deployment hands it: two workers in staging, twelve in production. The big clients' trades sit together at the end of the day's file and take far longer to net than the rest, and in production the job runs about as long as it would on one thread, while most workers sit idle near the end.

The operations team has set the rule for how the day is cut up: each worker of the pool the job runs in gets between 32 and 64 pieces of a large day, and a batch of minRows trades or fewer is netted whole, because a piece that small isn't worth a task.

SettlementJob already halves the day until a range fits in one piece. Finish leafSize(rows) in src/main/java/practice/SettlementJob.java, which says how many trades one piece may hold. About two lines in total. The tests run the job on real pools of one to forty workers.

When You Know What Each Item Costs, Split by Cost


Sixty-four leaves per worker fixed the time, but every leaf is sized for the options: the 3.8 million bonds are cut into the same small pieces. When the cost of each item is known, or can be estimated, the threshold can count steps instead of rows, and the split point can halve the steps:



With budget set to the total steps divided by the leaves wanted:



Eight leaves of equal work (175 ms) match 1,535 tasks split by count (163 ms). The bonds end up in a few large leaves and the options in many small ones, which a threshold in rows can't do with one number.


Two details guard the general case, though no position here comes near the budget. A single item can cost more than the whole budget, and a range of one item can't split, so to - from == 1 is a leaf too. And when the last item holds more than half the range's steps, the first index that reaches half is to itself; hi = to - 1 keeps the right half non-empty. Without it, the left half is the whole range again, and the task forks itself until the stack overflows.


The estimate only decides where to cut, so a poor one costs balance, not correctness, and a few leaves per worker on top of it absorb the error: four per worker by work ran in 142 ms.

The Split Point Sets the Depth: Halve, or Fork the Chunks From a Loop


An audit job walks a list in chunks of 1,000 items: 500,000 items in the test file, 8 million at year end. Its task forks the first chunk and handles the rest of the range the same way (ex/Peel.java):



The same items, halved instead, on a ForkJoinPool(8):



On the test file both are fast, because the other workers steal the chunks as they are forked. But compute() on the rest is a call on the same thread, so peeling adds a stack frame per chunk: 499 on the test file, and 7,999 needed on the year-end file, more than a worker's stack holds. Halving adds a level each time the range halves, 13 levels for 8 million items. Each steal of a half also carries half of what is left, so the thieves split it further on their own threads, and 46 steals were enough instead of one per chunk.


An uneven ratio deepens the tree far less than peeling. The module's SplittingAndJoining also has a 10/90 split, cut at start + length / 10. Over 8 million items at a threshold of 1,000 it went 86 levels deep and made 48,531 tasks, against 13 and 16,383 for 50/50, and ran in 47 ms against 53 (ex/Ratio.java). On even work it costs tasks, and the time doesn't show it.


When the chunks are known in advance, as in a list of files, fork them from a loop instead of recursing. ForkJoinTask.invokeAll(chunks) forks every chunk but the first, runs that one on the calling thread, then joins the rest, newest first. The stack stays one level deep whatever the count. Every chunk then sits on one worker's queue and costs a steal of its own, which is cheap while the chunks are few:



Over 8 million items, invokeAll with 8 to 80,000 chunks ran in 55 to 77 ms (ex/JoinOrder.java).

Summary


  • Uneven work needs many more leaves than workers. A started leaf can't be stolen, so the leaf that holds the expensive rows sets the job's time; four per worker wasn't enough when 5% of the rows held 91% of the work.
  • When you know what each item costs, split by cost. A threshold in steps and a split point at half the steps balance with few tasks; a single item is always a leaf, and the split point stays inside the range.
  • The split point sets the depth. Peeling a chunk at a time costs a stack frame per chunk and overflows on large inputs; halve, or fork known chunks from a loop.

Challenges


  1. Your positions are not sorted by type, and the options are spread through the file. Run Uneven with the options scattered: how many leaves per worker do you need now, and why?
  2. The pricing cost of an option depends on its maturity, and you only have an estimate. How wrong can the estimate be before split-by-cost is worse than sixteen leaves per worker by count?
  3. Replace the fixed threshold with getSurplusQueuedTaskCount() > 3 as the leaf test, and compare its task counts and times with yours on the uneven data.


Design Note: Why the Pool Can't Split a Leaf for You


A ForkJoinPool balances by moving tasks, and a task is opaque: once compute() is running, the pool sees a thread and a stack, not a loop with a range it could cut. Splitting a running leaf would need every task to report its progress and accept a new end while it runs. Fork/join keeps the contract small, a compute() that returns, and leaves the decision of how small a piece must be to the code that knows what its items cost. That code has to decide before the work starts, from an estimate, and no test fails when it decides wrong.
