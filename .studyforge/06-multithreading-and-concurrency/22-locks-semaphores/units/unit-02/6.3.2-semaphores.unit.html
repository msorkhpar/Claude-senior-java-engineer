The invoice service calls a tax API whose contract allows eight concurrent calls per client; a client that keeps going over is blocked for an hour. The team put the call behind a Semaphore(8).

Two incidents followed. In the first, the service stopped answering: 200 request threads were parked in Semaphore.acquire(), availablePermits() read 0, and the tax API saw no traffic from us. In the second, the provider blocked us for going over the limit, while our gauge showed availablePermits() at 13 on a semaphore created with 8.

Both had one cause: a semaphore is a counter that doesn't know who took a permit and believes every release() it is given.

Gate stands in for the tax API: it counts the callers inside call(), remembers the peak, and can time out its first few calls. Gate.load starts n callers, waits two seconds, and prints what happened.

A Semaphore Counts Permits: It Limits How Many Threads Are Inside, Not Which Ones

new Semaphore(3) holds three permits. acquire() takes one, and waits while there are none; release() puts one back and wakes a waiting thread. That number is the semaphore's whole state. Twelve callers through three permits to a 50 ms call:

Two things are often read into the count, and neither holds.

It doesn't make the guarded code exclusive. With three permits, three threads run the code between acquire() and release() at once. Six threads each increment a shared long 200,000 times inside the semaphore:

Only a single permit gives mutual exclusion. With more, the shared state needs its own protection, which is why the module's ResourcePool keeps its free connections in a ConcurrentLinkedQueue.

It doesn't limit a rate. Suppose the contract had said "ten requests per second". Ten permits in front of a 5 ms call, with forty threads calling for one second:

Each permit came back when its call ended and was taken again at once. Permits bound how many calls run at once, not how many start per second. A rate limit needs a clock: permits that come back on a schedule, not when the call ends.

A Semaphore Has No Owner and Trusts Every Release: Release Exactly What You Acquired

A ReentrantLock knows which thread holds it (unit Reentrant locks) and refuses an unlock() from any other thread. A semaphore records only the count:

So Semaphore(1) is not a lock. It isn't reentrant: a thread that holds the permit and asks again waits for itself, with a plain acquire() for ever. And any thread may give a permit back, whether or not it took one. A mistake doesn't throw; it moves the count, and the count is your limit.

A permit that isn't given back is gone. The tax call without a finally, through a Semaphore(3) to new Gate(50, 3), whose first three calls time out:

The three timeouts took the three permits with them, and every later caller waits in acquire() for ever. This is the first incident, and only a restart brings the permits back.

A release that didn't follow an acquire adds a permit. A refusal path written in a hurry, run in three waves of twelve callers against the same Semaphore(3):

Every refused caller gave back a permit it never took, raising the limit by one. This is the second incident. Refusals happen when the semaphore is full, when the provider is busiest, so the limit grew under the most load until the provider blocked us.

When the Permits Run Out, Choose: Wait, Wait a Bounded Time, or Refuse


One afternoon the tax API slows from 50 ms to two seconds a call. Twenty requests arrive together, and three permits are free. What happens to the other seventeen, one second in, depends on which call takes the permit:




acquire() waits as long as it takes, and every waiting thread comes from the server's request pool. With a pool of 200 and invoices arriving steadily, the pool fills within seconds, and requests that never touch tax, the health check among them, have no thread to run on. tryAcquire(200, MILLISECONDS) waits a bounded time and then returns false; tryAcquire() decides at once. Both freed the refused threads to answer "try again later".


Choose by the caller. A user waiting for a page can afford a short wait; a batch job that will retry anyway should be refused at once. An unbounded acquire() fits only where waiting is the point, as with the signals in the final section, or the release is sure to come soon.


Two shortcuts look like tryAcquire() and aren't.


Checking availablePermits() first. if (permits.availablePermits() > 0) permits.acquire(); reads the count, and by the time acquire() runs another thread may have taken the permit. Twenty threads make 100 calls each through Semaphore(2), skipping the call when the check sees no permit:



Seven calls saw a free permit and then waited anyway. availablePermits() is a snapshot for a gauge, never a decision; tryAcquire() checks and takes in one atomic step.


Expecting tryAcquire() to respect a fair semaphore. new Semaphore(n, true) hands permits to waiting threads in arrival order. tryAcquire() ignores that order, as its javadoc says: it takes a permit that is free at that moment, even with threads queued. A fair semaphore with one thread queued gets a release, and the main thread tries at once:



On a fair semaphore, tryAcquire(0, TimeUnit.SECONDS) refuses when another thread is queued ahead of it, so it keeps the order.

acquire(n) Takes All n Permits at Once or None


The video service's GPU runs at most four encoder sessions, guarded by Semaphore(4). A 4K transcode needs three. Taking them one at a time looks the same until two 4K jobs start together. A CyclicBarrier of two forces the unlucky interleaving: each job has two sessions before either asks for its third.




Each job holds two sessions and waits for one the other holds, so neither finishes. Without the barrier this happens only when the timing goes wrong, so it passes tests and shows up under load. acquire(3) waits until three permits are free at once and takes them together.


All or none holds for interrupts too. A job waiting in acquire(3) while two sessions are free holds none of them, and leaves holding none:



tryAcquire(3) is the same without the wait: three, or none and false. Give them back with release(3).


A Waiting acquire(n) Can Be Overtaken


All or none has a price on a non-fair semaphore, which new Semaphore(4) is. All four sessions are busy and the 4K job waits for three. Two sessions finish, and a 1-session job arrives:




On the non-fair semaphore the small job took one of the two sessions the 4K job was waiting for. With a steady stream of small jobs the free count may seldom reach three, and the big job can wait a very long time. A fair semaphore queues the small job behind the big one, at the cost of two sessions idle until a third frees up. When big requests must not starve, that trade is usually worth it.

    Share the GPU's encoder sessions between 1080p and 4K jobs
    

      

    • acquire(n) and tryAcquire(n) take all n permits together or none, including when interrupted, so a waiting job never holds part of what another needs.
      • 

    • On a non-fair semaphore a waiting acquire(n) can be overtaken by smaller requests; a fair one queues them behind it.

A Semaphore Keeps a Release Nobody Waited For, Which Makes It a Signal


The ownerless count also makes a semaphore a signal between threads. On startup the service must not take traffic until three caches have loaded, each on its own thread. Each loader releases a permit of a Semaphore(0) when done, and the starter waits for three. One loader finishes before the starter waits at all:




The early release raised the count to 1, and the starter's tryAcquire(3, ...) counted it. The Semaphore javadoc also states that actions before a release() happen-before actions after a successful acquire in another thread (unit Definition of happens-before relationships), so the starter sees what the loaders wrote.


A negative start needs one more release than it seems to. new Semaphore(-2) is legal, and is sometimes used so that a plain acquire() waits for several releases. But an acquire needs a count of at least 1:



To wait for N releases with acquire(), start at 1 - N, or start at 0 and call acquire(N).


Leftover permits carry over. The same semaphore is reused for the hourly reload. In round 1 a fourth, optional cache also signals, so four releases meet an acquire(3):



Round 2 started with round 1's leftover permit, so it let traffic in with two of its three caches loaded. Give each round its own semaphore, or use a CountDownLatch, whose count can't be reset.

Summary


  • A semaphore counts permits, nothing more. With N above 1, N threads run the guarded code together, so shared state needs its own protection; permits bound concurrency, not a rate.
  • A semaphore has no owner and trusts every release. A missed release shrinks the limit until the service stalls; a release on a path that never acquired (a refusal, or an interrupted acquire() inside the try) raises it. Acquire before the try, release in its finally.
  • When permits run out, the acquire call decides. acquire() ties up a thread while the dependency is slow; tryAcquire(timeout) and tryAcquire() give it back. Checking availablePermits() first is a race, and tryAcquire() jumps a fair semaphore's queue.
  • acquire(n) takes all n permits or none. n single acquires can deadlock two jobs; on a non-fair semaphore a waiting acquire(n) can be overtaken by smaller requests.
  • A release with no one waiting is kept. That makes Semaphore(0) a signal that can't be missed, and leftover permits carry over to the next use.

Design Note: Why a Semaphore Checks No Owner


A lock answers "who is inside?", and a semaphore answers "how many more may come in?". Java's Semaphore keeps only the number on purpose: the thread that frees a permit need not be the one that took it. That lets a loader signal a starter, or a callback on another thread give back a request's permit.


The cost is that misuse shows up weeks later, as a hung service or a blocked client, with nothing in the logs pointing to the line. So in application code, put a semaphore inside a class that knows what it handed out, such as a gateway with one call method that acquires and releases in one place, and don't pass the raw semaphore around.
